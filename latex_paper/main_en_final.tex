\begin{abstract}
	This study introduces a novel computational framework combining Non-Smooth Dynamics (NSD) and machine learning (ML) to optimize rotational friction dampers (RFDs) in steel moment-resisting frames (SMRFs). The NSD-LCP-PSOR method reformulates friction as a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP), eliminating tuning parameters while preserving Coulomb's exact friction law. A dataset of $\approx$434,000 nonlinear time-history simulations was generated for 1,000 shear-type frames (3-20 stories) with fundamental periods $T_1$ (0.3-3.0s) under 50 RFD configurations and eight real seismic records. Seismic performance was quantified using the composite index $CR_2$, which normalizes peak inter-story drift, peak floor acceleration, and base shear to the uncontrolled structure's response. ML regression models (XGBoost and Random Forest), trained via GroupKFold cross-validation, reduced prediction error by 49\% compared to linear baselines, achieving coefficients of determination ($\Rsq$) of 0.907 and 0.927 for median and 10th-percentile $CR_2$ values. The damper coverage density ($\etad=\nd/\np$, where $\nd$ is the number of dampers and $\np$ the potential locations) emerged as the dominant design variable ($\rho=-0.73$ with $CR_2$), with 80\% of maximum performance improvement achieved at $\etad\approx0.85$ for mid- and high-rise frames (6-20 stories), establishing a practical diminishing-returns threshold. The resulting ML surrogate models enable simulation-free optimization, providing robust design criteria across diverse structures and seismic inputs. Unlike prior single-structure studies, this framework generalizes recommendations: $\etad\geq0.60$ prevents structural damage while $\etad\geq0.85$ ensures serviceability by controlling displacements and accelerations. The recommendations demonstrate high robustness, with 67.9\% of structures requiring identical damper configurations under both median and 90th-percentile scenarios. Future work will extend the NSD-LCP-PSOR method to include soil-structure interaction and three-dimensional frame modeling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Earthquake-resistant design of steel structures relies on effective energy dissipation mechanisms to mitigate seismic demands. Rotational friction dampers (RFDs) offer a promising solution by redirecting seismic energy from structural members to non-structural frictional mechanisms. This approach protects primary elements while improving the predictability of seismic behavior compared to conventional systems where energy is dissipated through structural damage \citep{pall_friction_1980, pall_response_1982, jaisee_state---art_2021, de_domenico_editorial_2020}. Experimental studies have demonstrated their potential, showing up to 89\% energy dissipation and reductions of 89\% in lateral displacement and 41\% in base shear under optimized configurations \citep{Mualla2002}.

The optimal design of RFDs presents significant challenges due to the complex, nonlinear nature of dry friction and the high computational cost of traditional optimization methods. Current approaches have notable limitations. Simplified models fail to accurately capture stick-slip transitions \citep{lee_seismic_2025, Jarrahi2020jbe}, while metaheuristic optimization algorithms remain computationally expensive and limited to specific cases \citep{moreschi_design_2003, moghaddam_optimal_2022, Jarrahi2020jbe, Jarrahi2020sdee}.

This study introduces an integrated simulation and machine learning framework to address these challenges. The proposed approach builds upon the Non-Smooth Dynamics (NSD) method, which formulates Coulomb friction as a differential-algebraic inclusion to directly represent stick-slip behavior without approximation \citep{Namorado2022}. A novel NSD-LCP-PSOR method reformulates the friction problem as a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP) solved via Projected Successive Over-Relaxation (PSOR). This formulation transforms the friction problem into a system of linear equations with complementarity constraints, using an iterative solver that converges robustly for large-scale systems. The method eliminates case-specific tuning parameters while preserving the exact Coulomb friction law, and scales efficiently to thousands of configurations.

A comprehensive dataset of approximately 434,000 nonlinear time-history analyses was generated, covering 1,000 shear-type frames (3-20 stories) under 50 RFD configurations and eight real seismic records. Seismic performance was quantified using the composite index $CR_2$, which normalizes peak inter-story drift, peak floor acceleration, and base shear to the uncontrolled structure's response.

Given the high-dimensional and nonlinear nature of the RFD design space, machine learning (ML) was employed to analyze this problem \citep{Kazemi2022}. The present work characterizes seismic response across a statistically representative design space, summarizing performance through the 10th-percentile, median, and 90th-percentile of $CR_2$. The resulting ML models (XGBoost and Random Forest) expose the response surface across the full design domain, enabling derivation of practical design criteria including a damper coverage density threshold of approximately 0.85 and an effective friction moment range.

The paper is organized as follows: \cref{sec:theory} presents the theoretical background; \cref{sec:method} describes the methodology; \cref{sec:results} reports the results; \cref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications; and \cref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the conclusions.

\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Rotational Friction Dampers (RFDs)}
\label{sec:theory:rfd}

RFDs dissipate seismic energy through controlled sliding between contact surfaces. The device configuration proposed by \citet{Mualla2002} comprises a central rotating plate, two side plates, friction material discs, and a pre-tensioned bolt assembly (\cref{fig:rfd}). The bolt-induced compression generates stable frictional forces that produce hysteretic cycles whose enclosed area directly corresponds to the dissipated energy.

A hysteretic cycle represents the force--displacement relationship during a complete loading--unloading sequence, with its enclosed area quantifying the energy dissipated in each cycle (\cref{fig:histeresis}). Conventional steel members exhibiting inelastic behavior under cyclic seismic loading typically demonstrate degrading hysteretic responses: progressive reductions in peak strength and enclosed area occur due to accumulated damage, a phenomenon known as cyclic deterioration or pinching \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015} (\cref{fig:histeresis}b). 

In contrast, RFDs governed by Coulomb's friction law produce stable, rectangular hysteretic loops (\cref{fig:histeresis}a). The loop dimensions remain constant across cycles because the friction mechanism does not degrade, with the area determined solely by the friction moment $\Mf$ and the imposed displacement amplitude.

The RFD's central plate connects to the beam while the side plates attach to the brace or column. During seismic events, relative inter-story drift causes rotation of the central plate against the friction discs, dissipating energy that would otherwise induce structural damage \citep{kim_use_2011}. This mechanism effectively decouples energy dissipation from the primary structural system.

The damper's capacity is characterized by its maximum $\Mf$, which determines the energy dissipation capacity. The equivalent lateral force $f_\mathrm{RFD}$ transmitted to the frame by the RFD is given by:
\begin{equation}
	f_{\mathrm{RFD}} = \frac{\Mf}{\ha},
	\label{eq:Fafr}
\end{equation}
where $\ha$ represents the lever arm distance from the bolt axis to the point of force application. Experimental studies by \citet{Mualla2002} confirmed that the RFD behavior remains frequency-independent within the 2--7\,Hz range, validating the application of Coulomb's friction law for seismic energy dissipation modeling.

\subsection{Dynamic Analysis with Non-Smooth Dynamics}
\label{sec:theory:nsd}

The discontinuous nature of Coulomb friction, characterized by abrupt transitions between stick and slip states, presents significant challenges for conventional time integration schemes \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015}. Traditional numerical approaches typically employ one of two workarounds, each with substantial limitations. Regularization approaches replace the discontinuous sign function with smooth surrogates, such as bilinear or hyperbolic tangent approximations \citep{lee_seismic_2025}. 

While facilitating numerical integration, these methods introduce artificial compliance at the slip--stick transition, failing to capture the true abrupt nature of Coulomb friction. Equivalent-damping approaches, on the other hand, substitute the friction device with a viscous dashpot of matched energy dissipation \citep{Jarrahi2020jbe}. This simplification neglects the rate-independent nature of friction and cannot reproduce the characteristic rectangular hysteretic behavior.

Both approaches introduce modeling error at the slip--stick transition and require case-specific calibration, making them impractical for large-scale studies such as the ${\approx}434{,}000$ simulations conducted in this work. To address these limitations, this study employs the NSD method proposed by \citet{Namorado2022}, which reformulates the friction law as a differential-algebraic inclusion. This approach solves the resulting complementarity problem at each timestep, offering two direct advantages for the present study.

First, the NSD method provides theoretical fidelity by exactly reproducing the rectangular hysteretic loops and sharp slip--stick transitions of Coulomb friction without tuning parameters. This ensures that the simulation dataset accurately represents the true nonlinear physics, providing high-quality training data for the subsequent machine learning models. 

Second, the method achieves computational efficiency by casting the per-timestep problem as a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP) solved with Projected Successive Over-Relaxation (PSOR). Each timestep requires only a small number of sequential scalar updates, typically converging in fewer than 20 iterations, making the method fast enough to be applied to thousands of structural configurations.

The NSD-LCP-PSOR approach therefore offers an ideal compromise between physical precision and computational efficiency, making it particularly well-suited for generating the extensive simulation datasets necessary for the machine learning applications developed in this study.

The following subsections present a detailed examination of the method's theoretical and computational foundations. The analysis begins with a formal definition of the friction law governing RFD behavior. The equations of motion for structural systems equipped with RFDs are then established. This is followed by a transformation of Coulomb's friction law into a LCP. The subsequent section describes the solution method employing PSOR, along with a complete description of the NSD-LCP-PSOR procedure. Finally, the extension of this method to large-scale multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) simulations is addressed, enabling efficient analysis of the complex structural systems considered in this research.

\subsubsection{Friction Law}
\label{sec:theory:friction_law}

For an RFD connecting adjacent floors $i$ and $i+1$, its resisting moment $M_{\mathrm{RFD},i}$ follows Coulomb's friction law:

\begin{equation}
	M_{\mathrm{RFD},i} \in -\Mf \cdot \operatorname{Sign}\left(\dot{\varphi}_i\right),
	\label{eq:coulomb}
\end{equation}
where $\Mf$ represents the maximum friction moment defining the damper's capacity, and $\dot{\varphi}_i$ denotes the relative angular velocity between floors $i$ and $i+1$. The set-valued sign function is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\operatorname{Sign}(v) =
	\begin{cases}
		\{+1\}    & \text{if } v > 0, \\
		[-1,+1]  & \text{if } v = 0, \\
		\{-1\}    & \text{if } v < 0.
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:sign}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:coulomb} describes the relationship between the resisting moment and the angular velocity. When the device is slipping ($\dot{\varphi}_i \neq 0$), the resisting moment takes a constant value of $\pm\Mf$, depending on the direction of rotation. This corresponds to the kinetic friction regime where the frictional force opposes the motion with a constant magnitude.

When the device is at rest ($\dot{\varphi}_i = 0$), the resisting moment can take any value within the interval $[-\Mf, +\Mf]$, as defined by the set-valued nature of the sign function in Equation~\eqref{eq:sign}. This behavior corresponds to the static friction regime where the frictional force adjusts to maintain equilibrium until the applied moment exceeds the friction capacity.

This formulation accurately captures the non-smooth nature of Coulomb friction, including the abrupt transition between stick and slip states without any smooth approximation.

\subsubsection{Equation of Motion}
\label{sec:theory:eom}

The dynamic behavior of a $\np$-story structural system equipped with $n_a$ RFDs is governed by the following differential inclusion \citep{Namorado2022}:

\begin{equation}
	\bm{M}\,\ddot{\bm{u}} + \bm{C}\,\dot{\bm{u}} + \bm{K}\,\bm{u}
	\in -\bm{M}\,\bm{r}\,\ddot{x}_g(t) + \bm{H}\,\bm{Q}_f(t),
	\label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
where: $\bm{M}$, $\bm{C}$, and $\bm{K}$ represent the mass, damping, and stiffness matrices of the structure, respectively; $\bm{u}$ is the vector of ground-relative displacements; $\ddot{x}_g(t)$ denotes the ground acceleration time history; $\bm{r}$ is the seismic influence vector (a unit vector for shear-type frames); $\bm{H}$ is a mapping matrix that identifies which floors contain dampers; $\bm{Q}_f(t)$ is the set-valued friction force vector, derived from Coulomb's friction law.

For numerical implementation, the continuous-time differential inclusion is transformed into a discrete-time formulation. The set-valued friction forces are replaced by impulse forces $\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}$, computed at each time step through a LCP. The discrete equation of motion becomes:

\begin{equation}
	\bm{M}\,\ddot{\bm{u}} + \bm{C}\,\dot{\bm{u}} + \bm{K}\,\bm{u}
	= -\bm{M}\,\bm{r}\,\ddot{x}_g(t) + \bm{H}\,\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}/\Delta t.
	\label{eq:eom_disc}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{From Coulomb Friction to a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP)}
\label{sec:theory:lcp}

The discrete equations of motion (Eq.~\ref{eq:eom_disc}) and Coulomb's friction law (Eq.~\ref{eq:coulomb}) must be satisfied simultaneously at each timestep. This simultaneous satisfaction poses a fundamental challenge in computational mechanics: unlike standard dynamic systems where forces can be determined explicitly, the friction impulse $\lambda_r$ for each damper cannot be expressed through a conventional equation. This difficulty arises because Coulomb friction exhibits a non-smooth, set-valued force-velocity relationship that cannot be described by a single-valued function.

At every time increment, the physical behavior of each damper must conform to exactly one of two mutually exclusive states:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Slip state}: When the contact surfaces are sliding relative to each other, the friction impulse reaches its maximum physical capacity:
\[ |\lambda_r| = F^{\lim}_r \equiv \frac{\Mf\,\Delta t}{\ha} \]
In this state, the relative velocity at the device is non-zero and is entirely determined by the structural motion. The friction force opposes the motion with its maximum possible magnitude, which is why the impulse reaches its bound $F^{\lim}_r$.

\item \textit{Stick state}: When the contact is locked and no relative motion occurs, the relative velocity at the device is zero. In this case, the friction impulse can take any value within the interval $[-F^{\lim}_r, +F^{\lim}_r]$ that is required to maintain static equilibrium. The impulse does not necessarily reach the bound, as it only needs to balance the other forces acting on the system.
\end{itemize}

This mutually exclusive structure---where a variable must be either at its bound or satisfy an equality constraint, but never both simultaneously---is the defining characteristic of a \textit{complementarity condition}. For systems with bilateral Coulomb friction, determining the impulse vector $\bm{\lambda}$ that simultaneously satisfies both the equations of motion and the friction law is mathematically equivalent to solving an LCP:

\begin{equation}
	\bm{W}\bm{\lambda} + \bm{q} = \bm{0},
	\quad |\lambda_r| \leq F^{\lim}_r \equiv \frac{\Mf\,\Delta t}{\ha},
	\quad r = 1,\ldots,n_a.
	\label{eq:lcp}
\end{equation}

The LCP formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:lcp} involves two key components: the compliance matrix $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_a \times n_a}$ and the velocity vector $\bm{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_a}$. The compliance matrix $\bm{W}$ is constructed from the structural matrices and the free-velocity predictor as $\bm{W} = (\bm{H}^{\top}\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H})\,\Delta t$, where:

\begin{itemize}
	\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{---}
	\item the diagonal entry $W_{rr}$ represents the local flexibility at device $r$, indicating how a unit impulse applied at device $r$ affects the relative velocity at the same device;
	\item the off-diagonal entry $W_{rs}$ quantifies the mechanical coupling between devices $r$ and $s$, capturing how an impulse at one device influences the velocity at another device through the structural connectivity.
\end{itemize}

The right-hand side vector $\bm{q}$ contains the free inter-story relative velocities at each device, computed during the prediction stage of the algorithm. Solving this LCP involves finding the impulse vector $\bm{\lambda}$ that, when applied to the structure, brings all devices to a physically consistent state---either slipping or sticking---while satisfying the global equilibrium of the system. This approach efficiently captures the non-smooth dynamics of Coulomb friction without approximation, making it particularly suitable for large-scale simulations.

\subsubsection{Solution by Projected Successive Over-Relaxation (PSOR)}
\label{sec:theory:psor}

The LCP defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:lcp} is solved iteratively using the PSOR method, which represents a constrained extension of the classical Gauss-Seidel algorithm. The core idea behind PSOR is to decompose the $n_a$-dimensional LCP into a series of $n_a$ scalar subproblems, each of which can be solved analytically. After each update, the Coulomb friction constraint is immediately enforced by projecting the solution onto the admissible interval.

The PSOR algorithm proceeds by cycling through each of the $n_a$ dampers in sequence. For damper $r$, while keeping all other impulses $\lambda_s^{(i)}$ ($s \neq r$) fixed at their current values, the $r$-th equation of the LCP is solved to obtain an unconstrained impulse:

\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\lambda}_r =
	-\frac{q_r + \displaystyle\sum_{s \neq r} W_{rs}\,\lambda_s^{(i)}}{W_{rr}}.
	\label{eq:gstep}
\end{equation}

This equation represents the classical Gauss-Seidel update step, providing the exact solution for $\lambda_r$ that would satisfy the $r$-th equation of the system if there were no friction constraints. However, this unconstrained solution may violate the physical bounds of the friction force. Therefore, it is immediately projected onto the admissible interval:

\begin{equation}
	\lambda_r^{(i+1)} = \max\!\left(-F^{\lim}_r,\;\min\!\left(F^{\lim}_r,\;
	\tilde{\lambda}_r\right)\right).
	\label{eq:psor}
\end{equation}

The projection step is crucial as it determines the physical state of each device:

\begin{itemize}
	\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{---}
	\item if $|\tilde{\lambda}_r| > F^{\lim}_r$, the impulse is clamped to the bound, indicating that the device is in a slipping state;
	\item if $|\tilde{\lambda}_r| \leq F^{\lim}_r$, the unconstrained value is retained, indicating that the device is in a sticking state.
\end{itemize}

After projection, $\lambda_r^{(i+1)}$ immediately replaces $\lambda_r^{(i)}$ before proceeding to the next damper. This successive use of the most recently updated values, as opposed to the simultaneous updates of the Jacobi method, significantly accelerates convergence.

The PSOR method is theoretically guaranteed to converge for this class of bilateral LCP because the matrix $\bm{W}$ is positive semi-definite. Specifically, $\bm{W}$ is a Gram-type matrix of the form $\bm{H}^{\top}\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}$ scaled by $\Delta t$, where $\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}$ inherits the positive definiteness of the effective mass matrix \citep{Namorado2022}.

In practice, the iteration process is initialized with a warm-start using the solution from the previous timestep, $\bm{\lambda}^{(0)} = \bm{\lambda}_k$, which leverages the temporal continuity of the seismic response to reduce the number of required iterations. Convergence is declared when the infinity norm of the difference between successive iterations falls below a threshold of $10^{-6}$, i.e., $\|\bm{\lambda}^{(i+1)} - \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\|_\infty \leq 10^{-6}$. A safety limit of 20 iterations is imposed, although in practice, convergence is typically achieved in fewer than five iterations for the vast majority of timesteps.

\subsubsection{NSD-LCP-PSOR Procedure}
\label{sec:theory:procedure}

The NSD-LCP-PSOR algorithm advances the solution from time step $k$ to $k+1$ through a three-stage procedure, with the complete implementation detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:nsd-lcp-psor}. Before entering the time integration loop, several preliminary computations are performed to enhance computational efficiency:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\bm{M}} = \bm{M} + \theta\Delta t\,\bm{C} + (\theta\Delta t)^{2}\bm{K},
	\qquad
	\bm{A} = \bm{M} - \theta\Delta t\,\bm{C} - (\theta\Delta t)^{2}\bm{K},
	\label{eq:Mhat}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\bm{W} = \left(\bm{H}^{\top}\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}\right)\Delta t.
	\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}

The matrices $\hat{\bm{M}}$ and $\bm{A}$ are modified mass matrices used in the Newmark time integration scheme, where $\theta$ determines the time integration method (typically $\theta=0.5$ for the trapezoidal rule). The matrix $\bm{W}$ represents the compliance matrix for the damper degrees of freedom. Initial conditions are set to zero: $\bm{u}_0 = \dot{\bm{u}}_0 = \bm{\lambda}_0 = \bm{0}$.

\subsubsection*{Stage 1: Free-Velocity Prediction}
\label{sec:theory:free_velocity}

At each time step, before considering the effect of friction forces, the algorithm first computes the velocities that the structural system would naturally attain due to inertia, elastic forces, and seismic excitation. This prediction phase, often referred to as the "free-flight" or "drift" phase, provides a baseline velocity field that will later be corrected to account for friction forces. The free velocities are calculated using:

\begin{equation}
	\dot{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{free}} = \hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}
	\!\left[
	\bm{A}\,\dot{\bm{u}}_k
	- \Delta t\,\bm{K}\bm{u}_k
	- \theta\Delta t\,\bm{M}\!\left(\ddot{x}_{g,k+1}+\ddot{x}_{g,k}\right)\bm{r}
	\right].
	\label{eq:vfree}
\end{equation}

This equation represents a modified form of the Newmark time integration scheme, where: \(\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\) transforms forces into accelerations (or velocities in this context); \(\bm{A}\,\dot{\bm{u}}_k\) accounts for the current velocity state, modified by the time integration parameters; \(\Delta t\,\bm{K}\bm{u}_k\) represents the elastic restoring forces; $\theta\Delta t\,\bm{M}\!\left(\ddot{x}_{g,k+1}+\ddot{x}_{g,k}\right)\bm{r}$ captures the effect of ground motion excitation, where $\ddot{x}_{g}$ represent the ground accelerations.

The resulting \(\dot{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{free}}\) vector contains the velocities that each floor of the structure would have at time step \(k+1\) if no friction dampers were present. These velocities serve as a reference state from which the effect of friction will be determined in the subsequent stages.

Next, the algorithm calculates the free inter-story relative velocities at each damper location using:

\begin{equation}
	q_r = \dot{u}^{\mathrm{free}}_{f_r} - \dot{u}^{\mathrm{free}}_{f_r - 1},
	\qquad r = 1,\ldots,n_a,
	\label{eq:qr}
\end{equation}
where $f_r$ denotes the floor index of the $r$-th damper. This calculation determines the relative velocity between adjacent floors where each damper is installed. These relative velocities, denoted by $q_r$, represent the motion that would occur at each damper location if the dampers were not present to resist the motion. They serve as input to the LCP in the next stage, where the actual friction forces will be determined to counteract these relative motions.

\subsubsection*{Stage 2: Friction Correction (LCP-PSOR)}
\label{sec:theory:friction_correction}

Having computed the free relative velocities $\bm{q}$ in Stage 1, the algorithm now addresses the core challenge: determining the friction forces required to bring the system into a physically consistent state. This is achieved by formulating and solving an LCP using the PSOR method.

The LCP, defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:lcp}, is constructed using the relative velocity vector $\bm{q}$ as input. The solution process begins with a warm-start initialization, using the friction impulse vector from the previous timestep:
$\bm{\lambda}^{(0)} = \bm{\lambda}_k$

This initialization exploits the temporal continuity of the seismic response, significantly reducing the number of iterations required for convergence. The PSOR method iteratively solves the LCP to find the friction impulse vector $\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}$ that simultaneously satisfies: the equations of motion for the structural system and the Coulomb's friction law at each damper location.

The resulting $\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}$ represents the impulse vector that, when applied to the structure, brings all dampers to a physically consistent state—either slipping or sticking—in a globally balanced manner.

\subsubsection*{Stage 3: State Update}
\label{sec:theory:state_update}

With the friction impulses \(\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}\) determined in the previous stage, the algorithm proceeds to update the structural state variables for the next time step. This update phase incorporates the effect of friction forces on the system's dynamic response through two key equations:

\begin{align}
	\dot{\bm{u}}_{k+1} &= \dot{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{free}}
	+ \Delta t\,\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}, \label{eq:vupdate}\\
	\bm{u}_{k+1} &= \bm{u}_k
	+ \Delta t\!\left[(1-\theta)\,\dot{\bm{u}}_k
	+ \theta\,\dot{\bm{u}}_{k+1}\right]. \label{eq:uupdate}
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:vupdate} updates the velocity vector by adding the contribution of friction forces, where \(\Delta t\,\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}\) represents the change in velocity due to the friction impulses applied at each damper location. This term effectively corrects the free velocities computed in Stage 1 to account for the actual friction forces.

Equation~\eqref{eq:uupdate} updates the displacement vector using a weighted average of velocities at time steps \(k\) and \(k+1\), where \(\theta\) determines the weight given to each time step.

The updated state variables \((\bm{u}_{k+1},\dot{\bm{u}}_{k+1})\) and the impulse vector \(\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}\) are stored for use in the next time step. The time step \(\Delta t = 0.01\)\,s is selected to match the sampling interval of the seismic records, providing 30 points per period for the stiffest structures (\(T_1 = 0.3\)\,s) and satisfying the stability criterion \(\Delta t / T_1 \leq 0.10\) \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015}.

Finally, the friction force at floor \(i\) is computed from the impulse using:
\begin{equation}
	f_{\mathrm{RFD},i} = \frac{\lambda_i}{\ha\,\Delta t}.
	\label{eq:ffrict}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
	\caption{NSD-LCP-PSOR for multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) frames with RFDs}
	\label{alg:nsd-lcp-psor}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require $\bm{M},\bm{C},\bm{K}$; $\bm{H}$, $\bm{F}^{\lim}$; $\{\ddot{x}_{g,k}\}_{k=0}^{n}$; $\Delta t$, $\theta$
		\Ensure  $\{\bm{u}_k,\dot{\bm{u}}_k\}_{k=0}^{n}$
		\vspace{2pt}
		\State \textit{// Pre-computation (once)}
		\State $\hat{\bm{M}} \leftarrow \bm{M} + \theta\Delta t\,\bm{C} + (\theta\Delta t)^{2}\bm{K}$
		\State $\bm{A} \leftarrow \bm{M} - \theta\Delta t\,\bm{C} - (\theta\Delta t)^{2}\bm{K}$
		\State $\bm{W} \leftarrow \left(\bm{H}^{\top}\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}\right)\Delta t$
		\State $\bm{u}_0 \leftarrow \bm{0}$;\quad $\dot{\bm{u}}_0 \leftarrow \bm{0}$;\quad $\bm{\lambda}_0 \leftarrow \bm{0}$
		\vspace{2pt}
		\For{$k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$}
		\State \textit{// Stage 1: Free velocity prediction}
		\State $\bm{b}_k \leftarrow \bm{A}\,\dot{\bm{u}}_k - \Delta t\,\bm{K}\bm{u}_k - \theta\Delta t\,\bm{M}\!\left(\ddot{x}_{g,k+1}+\ddot{x}_{g,k}\right)\bm{r}$
		\State $\dot{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{free}} \leftarrow \hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{b}_k$
		\State \textit{// Stage 2: Free relative velocities}
		\For{$r = 1,\ldots,n_a$}
		\State $q_r \leftarrow \dot{u}^{\mathrm{free}}_{f_r} - \dot{u}^{\mathrm{free}}_{f_r-1}$
		\EndFor
		\State \textit{// Stage 3: PSOR corrector}
		\State $\bm{\lambda}^{(0)} \leftarrow \bm{\lambda}_k$
		\While{$\|\bm{\lambda}^{(i+1)} - \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\|_\infty > \varepsilon$}
		\For{$r = 1,\ldots,n_a$}
		\State $\tilde{\lambda}_r \leftarrow -\!\left(q_r + \sum_{s \neq r} W_{rs}\lambda_s^{(i)}\right)\!\big/W_{rr}$
		\State $\lambda_r^{(i+1)} \leftarrow \max\!\left(-F^{\lim}_r,\;\min\!\left(F^{\lim}_r,\;\tilde{\lambda}_r\right)\right)$
		\EndFor
		\EndWhile
		\State $\bm{\lambda}_{k+1} \leftarrow \bm{\lambda}^{(*)}$
		\quad\textit{($F^{\lim}_r = \Mf\Delta t/\ha$)}
		\State \textit{// Stage 4: State update}
		\State $\dot{\bm{u}}_{k+1} \leftarrow \dot{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{free}} + \Delta t\,\hat{\bm{M}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}$
		\State $\bm{u}_{k+1} \leftarrow \bm{u}_k + \Delta t\!\left[(1-\theta)\,\dot{\bm{u}}_k + \theta\,\dot{\bm{u}}_{k+1}\right]$
		\EndFor
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Extension of the NSD Method to Large-Scale MDOF Simulations}
\label{sec:theory:extension}

While the original NSD method proposed by \citet{Namorado2022} was validated for low- to mid-rise frames of one to five stories, the NSD-LCP-PSOR implementation presented in this study builds upon and extends previous work by \citet{Ponce2026}, which verified the method for a 10-story shear frame subjected to five real seismic records within a Monte Carlo probabilistic framework. The earlier study confirmed that the method produces hysteretic cycles and displacement responses consistent with established literature. To enable application to large-scale structures with up to twenty stories, three key adaptations were implemented:

1. \textbf{Joint LCP Formulation:} Unlike the original sequential approach, the current implementation solves the impulses of all dampers simultaneously using the compliance matrix $\bm{W}$. This matrix captures the mechanical interactions between devices through the structure, enabling efficient solution of the coupled system. The joint formulation is particularly advantageous for large-scale systems, as it accounts for the global interaction between dampers and avoids the potential inaccuracies of sequential solutions.

2. \textbf{Elimination of Tuning Parameters:} The original algorithm required a case-specific tuning parameter $\alpha$ to ensure convergence. By reformulating the problem as an LCP, this parameter is eliminated, significantly enhancing the robustness and generality of the method. This adaptation facilitates application to thousands of structural configurations without the need for parameter calibration, making the method particularly suitable for large-scale parametric studies.

3. \textbf{Warm-Start Initialization:} The solution from the previous time step is used as the initial guess for the current step. This warm-start approach exploits the temporal continuity of seismic responses, where structural behavior typically changes gradually between time steps. By providing a physically reasonable initial guess, this adaptation significantly reduces the number of iterations required for convergence, improving computational efficiency without compromising accuracy.

\subsection{Seismic Performance Metric}
\label{sec:theory:metrica}

Existing metrics for evaluating friction damper performance present limitations when assessing comprehensive structural behavior. Energy-based criteria, such as the dissipated-to-input energy ratio used by \citet{Jarrahi2020jbe,Jarrahi2020sdee}, quantify energy dissipation but fail to reflect structural demands. Inter-story drift ratios, while useful for structural safety assessment \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015}, neglect floor accelerations affecting non-structural components. Single-criterion metrics risk optimizing one parameter at the expense of others, potentially favoring configurations that reduce displacements while amplifying accelerations.

This study adopts the composite normalized index $CR_2$, integrating three key engineering demand parameters:

\begin{itemize}
	\item peak displacement ($R_1$): governing structural safety and residual drift;
	\item peak absolute acceleration ($R_2$): governing non-structural damage and occupant comfort;
	\item peak base shear ($R_3$): governing foundation demands.
\end{itemize}

Each component is normalized by the corresponding uncontrolled response, making $CR_2$ dimensionless and comparable across structures. Performance is measured using normalized ratios \citep{Namorado2022,Jarrahi2020jbe,Mualla2002}:

\begin{equation}
	R_1 = \frac{u_{\max}^{(\mathrm{c})}}{u_{\max}^{(0)}}, \quad
	R_2 = \frac{\ddot{u}_{\max}^{(\mathrm{c})}}{\ddot{u}_{\max}^{(0)}}, \quad
	R_3 = \frac{V_{\max}^{(\mathrm{c})}}{V_{\max}^{(0)}},
	\label{eq:R_individual}
\end{equation}
where superscript (c) denotes the controlled structure and (0) the
uncontrolled one. Composite indices combine these ratios:

\begin{equation}
	CR_1 = \sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2}, \qquad
	CR_2 = \sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2 + R_3^2}.
	\label{eq:CR}
\end{equation}

For uncontrolled structures, $CR_2 = \sqrt{3}$; lower values indicate improvement. Performance is summarized using three statistics across eight seismic records \citep{Kazemi2022}:

\begin{align}
	\CR{P10} &= Q_{0.10}\!\left(\{CR_2^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_r}\right), \quad
	\CR{med}  = Q_{0.50}\!\left(\{CR_2^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_r}\right), \notag\\
	\CR{P90} &= Q_{0.90}\!\left(\{CR_2^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_r}\right).
	\label{eq:CR2stats}
\end{align}
where $Q_p(\cdot)$ denotes the $p$-th sample quantile and $N_r = 8$ is the
number of seismic records.

The 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles represent favorable, typical, and conservative scenarios respectively. The $\CR{P90}/\CR{P10}$ ratio measures robustness: values near unity indicate consistent performance across records.

From an engineering standpoint, this three-statistic summary is consistent
with the multi-stripe analysis framework recommended by FEMA~P-58
\citep{FEMAP58} and adopted in recent seismic performance studies
\citep{Kazemi2022}: low percentiles represent seismic conditions favorable
to the damper (low-intensity or spectrally mismatched records where the
uncontrolled structure also performs well), while high percentiles represent
conservative conditions where demand is large and every unit of additional
control capacity matters. 

\subsection{Machine Learning for Structural Optimization}
\label{sec:theory:ml}

The goal is to learn a continuous mapping from design variables to seismic performance so that the response surface can be explored without running additional simulations. Because $CR_2$ is a real-valued engineering demand parameter rather than a class label, this is inherently a regression problem: a function $\hat{y} = f(\bm{x};\,\bm{\theta})$ is trained to predict each $CR_2$ statistic from the structural and damper configuration $\bm{x}$. Training minimizes the mean squared error (MSE):

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\bigl(y_i - f(\bm{x}_i;\,\bm{\theta})\bigr)^2.
	\label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}

The three targets---$\CR{P10}$, $\CR{med}$, and $\CR{P90}$---are predicted by independent models rather than a single multi-output model. This choice is deliberate: each percentile represents a structurally different relationship between design variables and seismic response. The 10th percentile captures favorable ground motion conditions, where a few critical decisions dominate; the median reflects typical seismic demand, governed by a broader set of variables; the 90th percentile represents the conservative scenario, where placement and friction moment effects are partially diluted by high input energy. Because these relationships differ, a shared model would systematically underfit at least one target. Independent models allow each to specialize in the structure of its own response surface, as confirmed by the divergent feature importance rankings in \cref{sec:results:importancia}.

Four algorithms are compared, deliberately spanning a complexity range from simple to complex: 

\begin{itemize}
	\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{---}
	\item Linear Regression and Ridge Regression \citep{Hastie2009} serve as linear baselines: strong baseline performance would imply that the design space is approximately linear and a response surface could be characterized with far fewer simulations; poor performance reveals genuine nonlinearity---a conclusion that cannot be drawn without the comparison.
	\item Random Forest \citep{Breiman2001} introduces nonlinearity and interaction effects by averaging $n = 100$ trees trained on bootstrap replicates of the data.
	\item XGBoost \citep{Chen2016} builds trees sequentially, each correcting the residuals of its predecessors.
\end{itemize} 

The contrast between these two ensemble strategies provides valuable insight: boosted models (such as XGBoost) tend to outperform when errors exhibit systematic patterns (i.e., when errors are consistent functions of the input variables), suggesting underlying nonlinear relationships in the data. In contrast, bagging-based models (such as Random Forest) demonstrate superior robustness when errors resemble random noise, indicating less structured variability in the input-output relationships.

A critical methodological challenge arises from the experimental design: each of the 1,000 structures contributes approximately 50 RFD configurations, so the same structure appears in many rows. Splitting data randomly would allow structure-specific patterns---eigenfrequency, mass distribution, stiffness profile---to appear in both training and validation folds, artificially inflating apparent generalization. To prevent this data leakage, generalization is assessed with five-fold GroupKFold cross-validation \citep{Kazemi2022}, with groups defined by structure identifier: all configurations of a given structure are always assigned to the same fold, ensuring the model is evaluated on structures it has never seen during training. This protocol measures the ability to generalize to new structures---the operationally relevant condition for a design-support tool.

The two evaluation metrics are the cross-validated Root Mean Square Error ($\mathrm{RMSE_{CV}}$) and the cross-validated coefficient of determination ($R^2_{\mathrm{CV}}$), where the subscript stands for cross-validated (CV), indicating that both quantities are averaged over the five GroupKFold folds:

\begin{equation}
	\RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{i \in V}\bigl(y_i - \hat{y}_i\bigr)^2},
	\qquad
	\Rsq  = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i \in V}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
	{\sum_{i \in V}(y_i - \bar{y})^2},
	\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the validation fold, $|V|$ its cardinality, $\hat{y}_i$ are out-of-fold predictions, and $\bar{y}$ is the sample mean of $y_i$ over $V$. Beyond predictive accuracy, feature importance from XGBoost---measured as the mean decrease in impurity (MDI) aggregated across all trees---ranks the contribution of each design variable to each target, providing interpretable design guidance: variables with high importance identify the dimensions of the design space where engineering decisions have the greatest leverage on seismic performance.

\subsection{Design of Experiments}
\label{sec:method:doe}

To ensure generalizable conclusions across a diverse building stock rather than a single structure, a comprehensive experimental design was implemented using randomized Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) \citep{McKay1979}. This approach, implemented via \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.LatinHypercube} \citep{Virtanen2020}, systematically explores the design space by dividing each factor's range into $N$ equal-probability strata and drawing one sample from each after random permutation. The scramble step enhances the design's low-discrepancy properties while maintaining uniform marginal coverage, avoiding the clustering issues of simple random sampling without assuming any response surface shape.

The experimental plan comprises 1,000 shear-type frames characterized by five key factors:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Number of stories $\np \in \{3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20\}$, spanning low- to high-rise building typologies.
	\item Fundamental period $T_1 \in [0.3, 3.0]$\,s, covering a wide range of structural flexibility.
	\item Mean floor mass $\bar{m} \in [150, 350]$\,t, representing typical building mass distributions.
	\item Mass profile: uniform, linearly increasing, decreasing, or concentrated at the top.
	\item Stiffness profile: uniform, linearly increasing, decreasing, or soft-story at the base.
\end{enumerate}

The stiffness vectors were analytically calibrated through an iterative scaling process of $\bm{k}$ until the solution to the eigenvalue problem:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The eigenvalue problem $(\bm{K} - \omega_1^2\,\bm{M})\,\bm{\phi}_1 = \bm{0}$ is solved to match the target period within 1\% tolerance, where $\omega_1 = 2\pi/T_1$ defines the circular frequency and $\bm{\phi}_1$ is the first mode shape.
\end{itemize}

For each structure, approximately 50 RFD configurations were generated to comprehensively explore the design space:
\begin{itemize}
	\item 10 deterministic configurations targeting specific coverage patterns (single floor, all floors, upper half, lower half, and combinations) to define the extremes of the placement space;
	\item 40 additional configurations generated via LHS over the binary placement space with friction moments $\Mf \in [25, 500]$\,kN$\cdot$m to explore the interior of the design space.
\end{itemize}

The presence of an RFD at floor $i$ is represented by a binary indicator $b_i \in \{0,1\}$, yielding a total damper count $\nd = \|\bm{b}\|_0$ and coverage density $\etad = \nd/\np$. The combination of 1,000 structures, approximately 50 configurations per structure, and eight seismic records results in a comprehensive dataset of 433,984 time-history simulations.

\subsection{Structural Models and Dynamic Analysis}
\label{sec:method:modelo}

Each of the 1,000 frames is modeled as a linear shear frame with lumped floor masses, following the approach adopted by \citet{Namorado2022}. By maintaining the frames in the elastic range, the analysis isolates the contribution of RFD placement and friction moment to the seismic response, avoiding confounding effects from structural nonlinearity \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015}. Structural damping is represented through Rayleigh damping with a damping ratio of $\zeta = 3\%$, calibrated to the first and third modes, which is consistent with lightly damped steel moment-resisting frames \citep{chopra_dynamics_2015}.

Seismic excitation is applied through eight historical accelerograms selected to represent a wide range of frequency content, duration, and geographic origin. The records include El~Centro~1940, Kobe~1995, Northridge~1994, Loma~Prieta~1989, Zacatula~1997, Jamiltepec~2018, Niltepec~2017, and Trinidad~1983. The use of multiple records rather than a single design spectrum is crucial for this study, as a single record could bias the performance ranking of damper configurations toward its specific frequency content. By using an ensemble of eight records and aggregating results into $\CR{P10}$, $\CR{med}$, and $\CR{P90}$ percentiles, the analysis captures the variability of the seismic environment. Each simulation is conducted with a time step of $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s using the NSD-LCP-PSOR integrator described in \cref{sec:theory:procedure}, with a fixed RFD lever arm of $\ha = 0.40$\,m \citep{Mualla2002, Jarrahi2020jbe}.

All seismic records are applied at their original, unscaled historical intensities without spectral matching or amplitude scaling. Consequently, the $CR_2$ metric quantifies relative performance under the actual intensity of each historical event rather than a uniform hazard level. This approach ensures that cross-record comparisons reflect the true variability of seismic demands across different earthquake characteristics.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:method:features}

While the raw design variables $(\np, T_1, \Mf, \nd)$ describe the basic structural properties and the number of dampers, they fail to capture the critical spatial dimension of damper placement, which significantly influences performance. To address this limitation, the input feature set is enhanced with:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textit{\roman*})]
	\item Four derived features that explicitly encode the geometric arrangement of dampers:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Coverage density $\etad = \nd/\np$: ratio of instrumented floors to total stories, identified as the dominant predictor in \cref{sec:results:importancia}
		\item Mean damper position $\bar{p} = \tfrac{1}{\nd}\sum_{i:\,b_i=1} i$: average floor index of damper locations
		\item Normalized mean position $\tilde{p} = \bar{p}/\np$: makes $\bar{p}$ comparable across buildings of varying heights
		\item Normalized vertical dispersion $\sigma_p = \mathrm{std}\!\left(\{i:b_i=1\}\right)/\np$: distinguishes between clustered and spread damper configurations
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item Twenty binary indicators $b_1,\ldots,b_{20}$: each $b_i$ equals 1 if floor $i$ carries an RFD, and 0 otherwise. For buildings with fewer than 20 stories, the remaining indicators are zero-padded to maintain consistent input dimensions across the dataset.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these 28 features (4 primary variables, 4 derived features, and 20 binary indicators) provide the model with comprehensive information about both the global structural properties and the detailed spatial distribution of dampers, enabling effective learning of the complex relationships between design choices and seismic performance.

\subsection{Training and Validation}
\label{sec:method:training}

Following the framework established in \cref{sec:theory:ml}, separate models are trained for each of the three performance targets ($\CR{P10}$, $\CR{med}$, and $\CR{P90}$). All four algorithms are trained on the same dataset of 29,000 configurations and evaluated using the identical GroupKFold cross-validation protocol, ensuring fair and direct comparison of model performances. To maintain objectivity and avoid overfitting during model selection, hyperparameters were fixed to widely accepted default values:
\begin{itemize}
	\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{---}
	\item Random Forest: 100 trees with maximum depth of 15.
	\item XGBoost: 100 estimators, maximum tree depth of 6, and learning rate $\eta = 0.1$.
	\item Ridge Regression: regularization parameter $\alpha = 1.0$.
\end{itemize}

No hyperparameter tuning was performed on the validation folds, ensuring that the reported metrics genuinely reflect out-of-sample performance.

Feature importance is assessed using the MDI criterion from the trained XGBoost models. For each internal node in each tree, the improvement in MSE, weighted by the number of samples reaching that node, is attributed to the feature used for splitting. These importance values are then averaged across all 100 trees in the ensemble. While MDI can be sensitive to high-cardinality features, the binary placement indicators ($b_i$) are relatively sparse in this dataset. Therefore, their individual importances are reported alongside the derived aggregate features ($\etad$, $\bar{p}$, $\sigma_p$) to facilitate comparison between detailed floor-level information and summary statistics. The feature importance rankings are computed separately for each target ($CR_2$ percentile), revealing how the relevance of design variables varies across different seismic scenarios, as discussed in \cref{sec:results:importancia}.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Variable Hierarchy}
\label{sec:results:importancia}

Understanding which design variables govern seismic performance is as
important as prediction accuracy: it translates a black-box model into
actionable engineering guidance. \Cref{fig:importancia} shows the 12 most
important predictors for each target according to the XGBoost MDI ranking.
For the typical and favorable scenarios, coverage density $\etad$ ranks first
in both $\CR{med}$ (MDI~=~0.670) and $\CR{P10}$ (MDI~=~0.766): higher
coverage density consistently reduces $CR_2$, confirming it as the dominant
design lever.

The binary placement indicator for Floor~1 (MDI~=~0.056) ranks second for
$\CR{med}$, reflecting the well-established finding that lower floors are
particularly effective given their larger inter-story drift demands
\citep{aydin_simple_2013, moghaddam_optimal_2022,
miguel_simultaneous_2016}. Friction moment $\Mf$ ranks third (MDI~=~0.052)
but interacts significantly with $\etad$, as discussed in
\cref{sec:results:interaccion}.

The importance hierarchy shifts substantially for the conservative scenario
$\CR{P90}$: $\etad$ drops to second place (MDI~=~0.111), displaced by the raw
damper count $\nd$ (MDI~=~0.311), while frame height $\np$ rises to third
(MDI~=~0.102). Under the most demanding records, the total number of devices
and the structural height become the primary governing factors, rather than
the normalized coverage fraction alone. This shift has a direct design
implication: for tall buildings under severe seismic demand, maximizing device
count proportional to height is more critical than optimizing placement
geometry.

\subsection{Diminishing Returns: Effect of Damper Coverage Density}
\label{sec:results:decreciente}

Knowing that $\etad$ is the dominant predictor raises a practical question:
how much coverage is enough? \Cref{fig:decreciente} shows the best achievable
$\CR{med}$ as a function of $\etad$, stratified by building height.
The relationship is consistently nonlinear across all height groups: gains are
largest in the range $\etad \in [0.30,\,0.60]$ and taper off progressively
beyond that point. To quantify the saturation objectively, a threshold was
defined as the coverage density at which 80\,\% of the maximum achievable
gain is reached. Mid- and high-rise frames (6--20 stories) cross this
threshold at $\etad \approx 0.85$, while low-rise frames (3--5 stories)
require $\etad \approx 0.95$ because each device covers a larger fraction of
the lateral system and removes a larger fraction of the remaining improvement
potential. The within-structure curves (\cref{fig:curvas_internas}) confirm
that this pattern is consistent across individual structures, not just at the
group level.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/F5_diminishing_returns.png}
	\caption{Diminishing returns in seismic performance as a function of coverage
		density $\etad = \nd/\np$, stratified by building height. Each curve shows
		the best achievable $\CR{med}$ (minimum over all configurations at that
		density), with the shaded band representing the interquartile range (IQR)
		across structures in each height group.}
	\label{fig:decreciente}
\end{figure}

A potential confound in this analysis is that structures with higher $\etad$
may also differ systematically in $\Mf$, placement geometry, or building
height, so that the observed relationship reflects those variables rather than
coverage alone. To rule this out, a stochastic dominance test was applied
across the eight seismic records: the 5th percentile of $CR_2$ in each
density interval was compared against the 95th percentile of the immediately
lower interval. Dominance is confirmed for seven of the eight records,
meaning that even a configuration unfavorable in $\Mf$ or placement but with
higher coverage outperforms 95\,\% of lower-density configurations.
Record-wise Pearson correlations range from $\rho = -0.74$ (El~Centro~1940)
to $\rho = -0.55$ (Kobe~1995), confirming the robustness of the finding.
The sole exception is Trinidad~1983 ($\rho = -0.18$): the relatively
low-intensity spectral content of this record reduces inter-story drift demand
to a level where placement and per-device friction capacity recover importance
relative to global coverage---a physical result consistent with the role of
$\etad$ as a demand-driven variable.

structure; the thick line is the median $\pm$ IQR. Dashed line: density at

\end{figure}

\subsection{Interaction Between Damper Count and Friction Moment}
\label{sec:results:interaccion}

While coverage density governs the first-order response, the friction moment
$\Mf$ is not a free parameter that can be set arbitrarily once coverage is
decided. \Cref{fig:mapa_calor} makes this interaction explicit: it maps
median $CR_2$ over a grid of binned $\nd$ and $\Mf$ values, stratified by
building height, revealing how the two variables reinforce or limit each other.

The clearest pattern across all height groups is that low coverage combined
with low friction ($\Mf \leq 100$\,kN$\cdot$m) consistently produces the
worst performance, while high coverage with moderate-to-high friction
approaches the optimum. The nature of the interaction, however, changes with
height. In low-rise frames (3--5 stories), $\nd$ and $\Mf$ contribute
approximately equally: increasing $\nd$ from 1--2 to 3--4 reduces $CR_2$
from 1.46 to 1.12, and increasing $\Mf$ from 100 to 300\,kN$\cdot$m yields
a comparable improvement at any fixed $\nd$. In mid- and high-rise frames,
the picture shifts: $\nd$ becomes the dominant lever, but $\Mf$ retains a
significant and consistent effect. Even at full coverage ($\nd \geq 11$), the
gap between $\Mf < 100$\,kN$\cdot$m and $\Mf > 400$\,kN$\cdot$m amounts to
0.46~$CR_2$ units in mid-rise frames. This confirms that $\Mf$ and $\nd$
are complementary, not substitutes: one cannot compensate for the other.

Optimal configurations across the dataset concentrate in the range
$\Mf \in [300,\,460]$\,kN$\cdot$m (median 400\,kN$\cdot$m,
IQR~[269,\,456]\,kN$\cdot$m) with coverage fractions close to unity,
providing a practical operating window for initial device sizing.

\subsection{Design Convergence Across Seismic Scenarios}
\label{sec:results:convergencia}

A key practical question is whether the optimal number of dampers changes
depending on which seismic scenario is used as the design basis. If the
typical and conservative scenarios lead to very different recommendations,
the designer would face an unresolvable trade-off between efficiency and
safety. \Cref{fig:convergencia} shows that this is not the case.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/F8_convergence_extended.png}
	\caption{Stability of the optimal damper count $\nd$ across seismic scenarios
		for 1{,}000 structures. Each point compares the optimal $\nd$ under the
		typical (median) and conservative (P90) scenarios; points on the diagonal
		indicate full agreement (67.9\,\% of structures), and color encodes
		building height~$\np$. Among diverging cases the median discrepancy is
		1.0~device.}
	\label{fig:convergencia}
\end{figure}

For 67.9\,\% of the 1,000 structures, the optimal $\nd$ is identical under
the median and P90 scenarios---the same number of dampers is recommended
regardless of whether the design is based on typical or severe seismic
demand. In the remaining 32.1\,\% of structures, the two scenarios disagree
by a median of one damper: 87.9\,\% of the diverging cases differ by at
most one device, and the mean discrepancy is 1.0~device. Crucially, P90
always recommends a $\nd$ equal to or greater than the median
recommendation---never fewer dampers. The conservative scenario therefore
acts as an upper bound on the median design, not as an alternative strategy
that would require a separate optimization.

Agreement rates by height group are 73\,\% for mid-rise frames (8--15
stories), 71\,\% for low-rise frames (3--7 stories), and 56\,\% for
high-rise frames ($\geq$16 stories) — the lower rate in tall buildings
reflects that the marginal benefit of one additional damper is smaller,
making the two scenarios more likely to sit on opposite sides of the
optimum. The practical implication is direct: design for the typical
scenario first, then verify against P90; in most cases no change will be
required, and when it is, adding a single device is sufficient.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Coverage Density as the Governing Design Parameter}

Among all design variables, coverage density $\etad$ emerges as the strongest
predictor of $CR_2$ ($\rho = -0.73$), and this dominance holds consistently
across all eight seismic records (\cref{sec:results:decreciente}). Translating
the 80\,\% maximum-gain threshold into actionable guidance, the analysis
recommends $\etad \geq 0.85$ for mid- and high-rise frames (6--20~stories)
and $\etad \geq 0.95$ for low-rise frames (3--5~stories). These thresholds
mark the point beyond which adding more dampers returns only marginal
improvement---a directly applicable cost-effectiveness criterion for
preliminary RFD deployment decisions.

\subsection{Interaction Between Friction Moment and Coverage}

Coverage density determines how many floors are instrumented, but the friction
moment $\Mf$ governs how much each device contributes. The two variables are
not independent: \cref{fig:mapa_calor} shows that at intermediate coverage
levels, doubling $\Mf$ at a fixed device count yields $CR_2$ reductions
comparable to adding two additional instrumented floors. In other words, a
designer who cannot install more devices can still recover much of the
performance gain by increasing the friction moment of the existing ones---up
to a point. Beyond $\etad \approx 0.75$, the sensitivity to $\Mf$ diminishes
and coverage becomes the binding constraint again.

This complementarity is precisely the kind of nonlinear interaction that
marginal correlation analysis misses and that motivates the ensemble ML
approach. As for the magnitude of $\Mf$, \citet{Jarrahi2020sdee} reported
an effective range of 81--439\,kN$\cdot$m for six dampers in a 10-story
steel moment-resisting frame (SMRF), and \citet{Ponce2026} identified
350--450\,kN$\cdot$m as the optimal range for the same structural type under
a probabilistic framework. The range found here, $\Mf \in [300,\,460]$\,kN$\cdot$m
(median 400\,kN$\cdot$m), sits squarely within both references, lending
independent cross-validation to the ML-identified optimum across a much
broader set of structural configurations.

\subsection{Comparison with Jarrahi et al.\ (2020): Energy vs.\ Displacement Criteria}

\citet{Jarrahi2020sdee} studied RFD performance in a 10-story SMRF and
reported that the energy dissipation objective saturates at
$\etad \approx 0.60$, the point at which frame yielding is effectively
suppressed. The present study finds saturation at $\etad \approx 0.85$---
25 percentage points higher. The discrepancy is not a contradiction; it
reflects the fact that the two studies measure different things. An
energy-based criterion asks: \emph{when does the frame stop yielding?}
The $CR_2$ criterion asks: \emph{when do displacement, acceleration, and
base-shear demands stop improving?} Because non-structural acceleration
demands ($R_2$) and base shear ($R_3$) continue to respond to additional
coverage beyond the yielding threshold, $CR_2$ detects saturation later.
The two thresholds are therefore best understood as complementary performance
levels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\etad \geq 0.60$: structural damage prevention, sufficient when
          the design objective is to protect load-bearing elements
          \citep{Jarrahi2020sdee};
    \item $\etad \geq 0.85$: serviceability control, required when the
          design must additionally limit acceleration demands on non-structural
          components, equipment, and building occupants.
\end{itemize}
A practitioner can use the first threshold as a minimum bound and the second
as the target when full performance-based objectives are in play.

\subsection{Robustness Across Scenarios}

\citet{Jarrahi2020jbe} and \citet{Jarrahi2020sdee} optimized RFD
configurations against individual seismic records, leaving open the question
of whether a design tuned to one record remains effective under a different
excitation. The multi-record framework adopted here addresses this directly:
by evaluating each configuration against eight historical accelerograms and
summarizing performance through P10, median, and P90 statistics, the
resulting recommendations are inherently robust to uncertainty in ground
motion selection. The convergence results (\cref{fig:convergencia}) confirm
this robustness quantitatively---67.9\,\% exact agreement and a median
discrepancy of one damper in the remaining cases---
showing that a single optimization pass against the typical scenario is
sufficient to identify a design that also performs well under severe demand.

\subsection{Limitations}

Five limitations bound the scope of the present conclusions.
First, the structural models are idealized elastic shear frames; frame
yielding and its interaction with the RFD response are not captured, so
the recommendations apply to demand levels where the frame remains elastic
or quasi-elastic. Second, the eight accelerograms, while geographically
diverse, may not represent all relevant tectonic environments, particularly
subduction zones with long-duration ground motions. Third, model validation
relies on cross-validation; an independent hold-out test set would provide a
more rigorous bound on generalization error. Fourth, a uniform $\Mf$ is
assumed across all devices in a structure; floor-specific friction moments
could further improve performance but would substantially expand the design
space. Fifth, no installation cost function is included, so the
recommendations maximize seismic performance without penalizing the number
of devices---an economic optimization layer remains as future work.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This study presented an integrated framework combining Non-Smooth Dynamics and machine learning to optimize rotational friction dampers in steel moment-resisting frames. The analysis of approximately 434,000 nonlinear time-history simulations across 1,000 diverse structural configurations and eight real seismic records yielded several significant findings.

The developed machine learning surrogate models demonstrated high predictive accuracy. XGBoost achieved coefficients of determination ($\Rsq$) of 0.907 and 0.927 for median and 10th-percentile $CR_2$ values, respectively, while Random Forest reached $\Rsq = 0.786$ for the 90th-percentile $CR_2$. Ensemble models reduced prediction error by approximately 49\% compared to linear baselines, quantifying the nonlinear relationship between design parameters and seismic performance.

A key finding emerged regarding the diminishing returns of damper coverage density. Within-structure analysis revealed that 80\% of the maximum $CR_2$ reduction is achieved at $\etad \approx 0.85$ for mid- and high-rise frames (6-20 stories) and at $\etad \approx 0.95$ for low-rise frames (3-5 stories). These thresholds provide practical cost-effectiveness criteria for RFD deployment. The optimal friction moment $\Mf$ identified through machine learning concentrates in the [300, 460] kN·m range (median 400 kN·m). For coverage densities $\etad \geq 0.75$, the $CR_2$ performance metric shows relative insensitivity to specific $\Mf$ values within the [200, 500] kN·m range, indicating that coverage fraction represents the most critical design parameter.

The established performance thresholds present an interesting comparison with previous energy-based criteria. While \citet{Jarrahi2020sdee} reported saturation of energy dissipation objectives at $\etad \approx 0.60$ (indicating elimination of structural yielding), the displacement-based $CR_2$ criterion employed in this study finds saturation at $\etad \approx 0.85$. This 25 percentage-point difference reflects the greater sensitivity of $CR_2$ to displacement and acceleration demands that persist beyond the yielding threshold. The two criteria thus describe complementary performance levels: $\etad \geq 0.60$ targets structural damage prevention, while $\etad \geq 0.85$ additionally ensures serviceability by controlling residual displacements and accelerations.

The robustness of the proposed design criteria was demonstrated through cross-scenario analysis. 67.9\% of structures received identical damper count recommendations under both median and 90th-percentile seismic scenarios, with a median discrepancy of only one damper in the remaining cases. This indicates that designs optimized for median seismic conditions provide near-optimal protection across the full variability of the eight-record ensemble.

Several avenues for future research emerge from this work. Three immediate extensions of the NSD-LCP-PSOR simulation engine are particularly promising. Incorporating soil-structure interaction would enable the foundation compliance to modify the effective input motion and frequency content experienced by the dampers, a critical consideration for soft soils and tall buildings \citep{jarrahi_soil-structure_2022}. Extending the current shear-frame model to three-dimensional frames would capture torsional coupling and bidirectional excitation effects, potentially shifting optimal damper placement patterns for asymmetric buildings. Replacing elastic frame elements with nonlinear formulations would permit simulation of high-intensity scenarios where frame yielding interacts with RFD response, addressing the primary source of epistemic uncertainty in the current results.

Beyond simulation enhancements, future work should explore floor-specific friction moment distributions, installation cost constraints, and broader seismic record suites representing different tectonic environments. The integration of trained surrogate models into interactive design tools represents another valuable direction for practical implementation.
